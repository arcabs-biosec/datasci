A7. Simple linear regression

x <- reg_data$elevation   # independent variable
y <- reg_data$SPPTotal    # dependent variable

## Means
xbar <- mean(x)
ybar <- mean(y)

## Slope
b <- sum((x - xbar) * (y - ybar)) /
     sum((x - xbar)^2)

## Intercept
a <- ybar - b * xbar

cat("Regression Equation:\n")
## Regression Equation:
cat("SPPTotal =", round(a, 4), "+ (", round(b, 6), ") x elevation\n")
## SPPTotal = 4.1757 + ( -0.003723 ) x elevation
## Predicted values
yhat <- a + b * x

## Sum of Squared Errors and Total Sum of Squares
SSE <- sum((y - yhat)^2)
SST <- sum((y - ybar)^2)

## Coefficient of determination
R2 <- 1 - (SSE / SST)
cat("R-squared:", round(R2, 4), "\n")
## R-squared: 0.1916
results_A <- data.frame(
  Elevation = round(x, 1),
  Observed  = y,
  Predicted = round(yhat, 2)
)

Interpretation

Based on a preliminary check using the Elev field already present in the csv, the slope of the elevation–richness relationship comes out negative (b ≈ -0.0038) with R² ≈ 0.19 (p ≈ 0.002, n = 49). Therefore, as elevation increases, species richness tends to decrease, and this relationship is statistically significant. However, elevation alone only explains around 19% of the variation in richness, thus, elevation is a relevant but incomplete predictor.

B4. Which relationship is statistically significant?

Using the same variables extracted from the sample data, the multiple regression model as a whole is significant (F ≈ 6.7, p ≈ 0.0008, R² ≈ 0.31, adjusted R² ≈ 0.26, n = 49). Looking at the individual predictors:

  • Temperature — significant (p ≈ 0.03), negative coefficient: sites with cooler mean annual temperature tend to have higher species richness.
  • Precipitation — significant (p ≈ 0.02), negative coefficient: sites with lower annual precipitation tend to have higher species richness (this dataset may be reflecting that drier, cooler high-elevation forest sites here happen to hold richer amphibian/reptile assemblages).
  • Elevation — not significant on its own in the multiple model (p ≈ 0.23), even though it was significant in the simple regression in Part A.

Elevation, temperature, and precipitation are strongly correlated with each other, since temperature and precipitation are largely elevation-driven. Once temperature and precipitation are in the model, they explain the most what elevation alone was reflecting in Part A, so elevation’s unique contribution is no longer statistically distinguishable from zero. In conclusion, elevation is still ecologically important, but its effect on richness in this dataset appears to act mainly through the climate variables (temperature and precipitation) rather than as an independent driver.
